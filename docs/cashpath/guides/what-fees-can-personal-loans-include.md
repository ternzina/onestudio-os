# What Fees Can Personal Loans Include?

Personal loans can involve charges in addition to interest, but the exact fees depend on the provider, product, agreement, and circumstances. Before deciding, identify what a fee is called, how much it is, when it is charged, whether it is optional or required, whether it reduces the cash received, whether it affects the broader disclosed borrowing cost, and what happens if a payment is late.

Those questions help turn a short offer summary into a clearer comparison. Start with CashPath's [Rates & Fees](/p/rates-fees) page, then keep the provider's disclosures nearby. CashPath is not a lender and does not determine loan fees, APR, interest rates, approval, or funding.

## Why personal loan fees matter

Interest is not necessarily the only borrowing cost. Depending on the provider and agreement, fees can affect total cost, the proceeds received, and the comparison between two offers. A fee should be considered with the interest rate, APR where disclosed, payment schedule, term, and total scheduled repayment.

Do not assume every fee affects APR or that every provider uses the same fee structure. A practical comparison looks at the complete offer. [How to Compare Personal Loan Offers](/p/how-to-compare-personal-loan-offers) explains how to put those details side by side.

## Origination fees

An origination fee is associated with originating or making a loan. Some providers may charge one. Depending on the agreement, it may be charged separately or deducted from proceeds. The actual disclosure explains the fee's purpose, timing, and effect on the transaction.

When reviewing an origination fee, compare the loan amount, fee, net proceeds, repayment obligation, and APR where disclosed. Do not assume an origination fee is always a percentage, always deducted upfront, or included in APR. The provider's documents for the offer in front of you are the source of truth.

## Documentation fees

The CFPB identifies a documentation fee as a fee for processing loan paperwork. Not every provider charges one. If it appears, check the amount, stated purpose, timing, and whether it is separate from another fee.

Avoid guessing from the name alone. Ask where the charge appears in the disclosure and whether it changes the money you receive or repay. The CFPB's [personal installment loan fee guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/do-personal-installment-loans-have-fees-en-2120/) is a useful starting point for questions to ask.

Keep related charges separate in your notes. A documentation fee and an origination fee may have different names, purposes, or timing. If a disclosure combines terms or uses unfamiliar language, ask for a plain explanation before accepting. Comparing written disclosures rather than relying on a verbal summary helps make sure the same question is answered for each offer.

## Credit and disability insurance charges

The CFPB lists credit insurance and disability insurance among possible personal installment loan charges and describes these products as generally optional. That does not mean they are optional in every transaction or jurisdiction.

Read the actual disclosures to determine whether coverage is offered, whether it is optional, what it costs, what it covers, and whether you affirmatively chose it. This guide does not provide insurance advice. A consumer can pause and ask the provider for an explanation when a charge or add-on is unclear.

## Non-filing insurance on secured loans

The CFPB identifies non-filing insurance as a possible charge when a loan is secured by collateral. This is a narrow example, not a statement that all personal loans are secured or that all secured personal loans have this charge.

If a disclosure mentions collateral or non-filing insurance, read the agreement carefully and ask the provider how that particular charge applies. Do not assume it applies simply because another loan used a different structure.

## Late fees

A lender may impose late fees according to the agreement. Before accepting, check when a payment is considered late, whether a late fee applies, the amount or method disclosed, and the consequences described for missed payments.

Do not assume a grace period or a universal late-fee amount. A payment plan should fit your budget before you agree to it. CashPath's [Responsible Lending](/p/responsible-lending) page offers general reminders about repayment ability and reviewing terms.

## Fees deducted from loan proceeds

The gross loan amount and the net amount actually received can differ. If a disclosed fee is deducted before funds are delivered, a borrower may care about both figures: the amount stated in the loan and the cash available after the deduction.

For example, Offer A might have an origination charge deducted from proceeds. Offer B could have a different fee structure with no comparable deduction. Do not compare only the headline loan amount. Compare what you receive, what you are expected to repay, and the terms that connect them. [How to Compare Personal Loan Offers](/p/how-to-compare-personal-loan-offers) provides a fuller checklist.

## Which fees are reflected in APR?

APR is broader than the interest rate and is intended to capture more of the borrowing cost. Certain finance charges can affect APR. But APR does not necessarily include every possible fee or charge that might appear in an agreement.

The actual disclosures govern the specific transaction. For a foundation, see [What Is APR on a Personal Loan?](/p/what-is-apr-on-a-personal-loan) and [APR vs. Interest Rate](/p/apr-vs-interest-rate). These figures are useful comparison tools, not a replacement for reading the fee section.

## Optional charges versus required charges

A fee or add-on can have very different significance depending on whether it is required for the loan or is an optional product or service. Insurance examples show why that distinction matters: the provider's written disclosures should identify what is offered and what applies.

Check the agreement rather than making a legal conclusion from the label alone. If declining an optional product could change the terms, ask the provider to explain the effect before deciding.

## A fee is not automatically a scam

Not every application-related or upfront charge is fraud. The FTC notes that legitimate lenders can sometimes require an application or appraisal fee. The red flag is a demand for money in exchange for a promise or guarantee that credit will be provided.

Be cautious about suspicious pressure, unexpected messages, or a provider identity that is difficult to verify. The FTC's [advance-fee loan guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-advance-fee-loans) explains this distinction. This is not a full scam guide, but it is a reason to slow down before paying for a guarantee.

## Questions to ask about a personal loan fee

Use these questions for each fee shown in an offer:

- What is the fee called, how much is it, and why is it charged?
- When is it charged, and is it optional or required?
- Is it deducted from proceeds, and where is it shown in the agreement?
- Does declining an optional product change the loan terms?
- Does the APR shown by the provider reflect this charge?
- Could it apply later, such as after a late payment?
- Who receives the fee, and who can explain it if the disclosure is unclear?

These questions do not require a provider to answer in a particular format. They help a consumer recognize what still needs clarification.

## How to compare two offers with different fees

Offer A might have a lower stated interest rate plus an origination or documentation charge. Offer B might have a different rate and a different fee structure. Neither headline alone establishes which offer fits better.

Compare APR, net proceeds, scheduled payment, term, fees, and total scheduled repayment where disclosed. Review the actual provider agreement, then use [Rates & Fees](/p/rates-fees) and [How to Compare Personal Loan Offers](/p/how-to-compare-personal-loan-offers) as supporting resources.

## Can you avoid personal loan fees?

It depends on available providers, loan products, eligibility, and the specific offer. A consumer may compare offers and ask about unclear or optional charges. That is different from a promise that a fee-free loan is available or that every fee can be negotiated.

The goal is not to find a single label that makes an offer good or bad. It is to understand the fee structure and decide whether the complete agreement makes sense for your circumstances.

Personal installment loans are generally repaid in scheduled installments, but product terms can differ. The CFPB's [overview of personal installment loans](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-personal-installment-loan-en-2114/) notes that rates may be fixed or adjustable depending on the provider and product. That is another reason to compare the complete disclosure, not just a single charge.

## The bottom line

Fee structures vary. Read the disclosures, understand each fee's purpose and timing, compare net proceeds with total borrowing cost, and distinguish optional add-ons from required charges. Do not pay for a promise of guaranteed credit.
