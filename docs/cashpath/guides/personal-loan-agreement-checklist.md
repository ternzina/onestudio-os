# Personal Loan Agreement Checklist: What to Review Before Signing

**Important:** CashPath is not a lender and does not create, approve, service, or change a provider’s loan agreement. This page is general financial education, not legal advice. The provider’s disclosures and signed agreement control the transaction.

## Short Answer

Do not sign a personal loan agreement because the monthly payment looks manageable or because the provider says the process is almost finished.

Before accepting, slow the decision down long enough to answer four questions:

1. **Who is the actual provider?**
2. **What will the credit cost in dollars and over time?**
3. **What exactly are you promising to pay, and when?**
4. **What happens if something changes, such as a late payment, early payoff or automatic debit problem?**

For covered closed-end consumer credit, federal Regulation Z requires creditors to provide specified disclosures. Depending on the transaction, these can include items such as the amount financed, finance charge, annual percentage rate (APR), payment schedule and total of payments. Not every disclosure item applies identically to every transaction, and some credit products follow different disclosure rules.

The safest habit is simple: compare the provider’s written disclosures with what you thought you were agreeing to before you accept.

## Step 1: Confirm Who Is Actually Offering the Credit

Online borrowing journeys can involve more than one company.

A website may be:

- a lender;
- a bank or credit union;
- a marketplace;
- a referral or lead-generation service;
- a technology platform; or
- a servicer involved after the loan is made.

CashPath is a request/referral starting point. It is not the lender and does not set APR, fees, eligibility, loan amount or repayment terms.

Before accepting an agreement, identify the company that would actually extend the credit. Check the name on the disclosures and agreement, and keep a copy of the contact information.

If the company name on the agreement is different from the website or brand you started with, do not assume that is automatically a problem. But do make sure you understand which company is legally responsible for the offer and which company you must contact later about payments or servicing.

## Step 2: Find the Amount Financed and the Amount You Will Actually Receive

A headline amount is not always the same as the amount that reaches your bank account.

For applicable closed-end credit, Regulation Z includes disclosure of the **amount financed**, which is a defined credit-disclosure concept. The CFPB’s official materials caution that amount financed should not simply be assumed to equal the note amount, proceeds or principal in every transaction.

Before accepting, compare:

- the requested amount;
- the amount offered;
- the amount financed shown in the disclosure;
- any fees deducted from proceeds;
- any fees financed into the obligation; and
- the amount you expect to receive.

If those numbers differ, understand why.

## Practical check

Write the figures on one line:

**Requested:** $_____  
**Offered:** $_____  
**Amount financed:** $_____  
**Expected cash proceeds:** $_____  
**Upfront/deducted fees:** $_____

Do not continue until the differences make sense.

## Step 3: Read the APR and Finance Charge Together

APR and interest rate are related but not identical concepts.

APR is designed as an annualized cost measure for credit and may reflect certain finance charges beyond stated interest. The **finance charge**, where applicable, is disclosed as a dollar amount representing the cost of credit under Regulation Z.

When comparing offers, do not choose using only:

- the smallest monthly payment;
- the lowest advertised interest-rate number; or
- the largest amount offered.

Instead, review the APR, finance charge, fees, term, payment schedule and total of payments together.

For more background, see **Rates & Fees** and **APR vs. Interest Rate**.

## Step 4: Read the Entire Payment Schedule

The payment schedule is the part of the agreement that turns a loan into a household obligation.

Confirm:

- how many payments are scheduled;
- how much each payment is;
- when the first payment is due;
- how often payments are due;
- whether the final payment differs;
- whether the rate or payment can change; and
- which payment method is expected.

Do not assume every personal loan uses the same calendar or that every payment will remain identical.

The CFPB describes personal installment loans generally as closed-end loans repaid in installments, but actual payment mechanics depend on the product and agreement.

## Budget test before signing

Take the scheduled payment and place it into a normal month that already includes:

- housing;
- utilities;
- food;
- transportation;
- insurance;
- child or dependent costs;
- existing debt payments; and
- irregular expenses that do not arrive every month.

An amount being offered does not mean the payment is comfortable for your household.

## Step 5: Look for the Total of Payments

For transactions where it is required, the **total of payments** represents the sum of scheduled payments disclosed under Regulation Z.

It can be one of the most useful numbers on the page because it turns a stream of monthly payments into a single total.

Ask:

- How much am I receiving?
- How much will I pay in scheduled payments if I follow the agreement?
- How much of the difference comes from interest or finance charges?
- Are there additional costs that are conditional rather than built into the scheduled total?

Do not confuse a low monthly payment with a low total cost. A longer term can reduce the regular payment while increasing the amount paid over time.

## Step 6: Identify Every Fee That Could Apply

A provider may charge fees depending on the product and circumstances.

Possible examples include:

- origination or processing-related charges;
- documentation-related charges;
- late-payment fees;
- returned-payment or insufficient-funds-related charges; and
- other costs disclosed for the specific transaction.

That list does not mean every personal loan includes every fee.

The CFPB advises consumers considering personal installment loans to check the lender’s disclosures and loan documents to understand which fees apply.

## Fee checklist

For every fee shown, record:

**Fee name:** __________  
**Amount or calculation:** __________  
**When charged:** __________  
**Deducted from proceeds, financed or paid separately:** __________  
**Can it be avoided by behavior?** __________

Then compare the full cost, not merely the advertised rate.

## Step 7: Read the Prepayment and Early-Payoff Language

Do not assume that paying early works the same for every loan.

For covered closed-end credit, Regulation Z includes disclosure requirements concerning whether a charge may be imposed for paying all or part of the obligation early.

Before signing, look for:

- any prepayment charge language;
- how interest is calculated;
- how extra payments are applied;
- how to request an official payoff amount; and
- whether the provider requires a particular payoff method.

If you later want to pay early, ask the actual provider or servicer for the current payoff amount rather than simply sending what appears to be the remaining principal.

See **Can You Pay Off a Personal Loan Early?** for a deeper checklist.

## Step 8: Check Whether Any Collateral Is Involved

If the agreement is secured by collateral, identify the exact asset and the provider’s rights under the agreement.

Questions to ask include:

1. What asset secures the loan?
2. Who must own the asset?
3. Are title, lien, insurance or valuation steps required?
4. What events can put the collateral at risk?
5. What notice or cure language appears in the agreement?
6. How is the lien or security interest released after payoff?

Do not accept a secured agreement until you understand what could happen to the pledged asset after default.

See **Secured vs. Unsecured Personal Loan** for more context.

## Step 9: Understand Automatic-Payment Authorization

Electronic payments can be convenient, but convenience is not a reason to skip the authorization language.

Check:

- which account may be debited;
- the expected amount and frequency;
- whether amounts can vary;
- when notice is provided for changes where applicable;
- how to update payment information;
- how to cancel or change authorization under the agreement and applicable law; and
- what happens if a debit is returned.

Keep automatic-payment authorization separate in your mind from the underlying obligation. Changing a payment method does not erase a debt you validly owe.

Federal Regulation E generally prohibits a creditor from conditioning an extension of credit on repayment by preauthorized recurring electronic fund transfers, subject to specified exceptions. A creditor can still offer optional automatic payment arrangements, and certain incentives may be permitted. Review the actual authorization and available repayment methods rather than assuming autopay is mandatory or prohibited in every situation.

## Step 10: Read Late-Payment, Default and Collection Terms Before You Need Them

The worst time to discover a contract term is after a missed payment.

Locate the sections covering:

- late fees;
- grace periods, if any;
- returned payments;
- delinquency;
- default;
- acceleration, if included;
- collection costs, if included and permitted;
- credit reporting; and
- how to request help or ask about hardship options.

Exact timing and consequences vary by provider, agreement and applicable law. Do not rely on a generic internet timeline.

If you are already worried that a payment may be late, see **What Happens If You Miss a Personal Loan Payment?** and contact the actual provider promptly.

## Step 11: Compare the Written Terms With What You Expected

Before clicking “accept,” write down what you believed the offer was:

- amount: ______
- APR: ______
- fees: ______
- term: ______
- payment: ______
- payment frequency: ______
- total repayment: ______
- collateral: yes / no
- automatic debit: yes / no

Then compare that list with the written disclosures.

If something changed, stop and understand the change.

Common surprises can include:

- lower proceeds than expected because of a deducted fee;
- a longer repayment term;
- a different APR than an earlier estimate;
- optional products added to the paperwork;
- collateral language;
- a different payment due date; or
- a hard credit inquiry authorization at a later stage.

A preliminary estimate is not the same thing as the final agreement.

## Step 12: Save the Documents

Before or immediately after acceptance, save copies of:

- the final disclosure;
- the signed agreement;
- the payment schedule;
- any automatic-payment authorization;
- provider contact information; and
- any confirmation page or email showing the final transaction.

Do not rely on being able to reconstruct the exact terms from memory later.

## A Five-Minute Final Review

If you are short on time, do not skip review entirely. Use this compressed version:

## Minute 1: Identity
Who is the actual creditor/provider?

## Minute 2: Cost
What are the APR, finance charge, fees and total of payments?

## Minute 3: Schedule
How much, how often and for how long will you pay?

## Minute 4: Risk
What happens after a late payment, early payoff, returned debit or default? Is collateral involved?

## Minute 5: Fit
Can the payment fit your real budget without depending on another loan to make the payment?

If any answer is unclear, do not treat urgency as a reason to accept.

## Bottom Line

A personal loan agreement is not paperwork to rush through. It is the document that turns an offer into a repayment obligation.

Before signing, verify the provider, understand the amount financed and proceeds, read the APR and finance charge, review the full payment schedule and total of payments, identify fees, check prepayment/default/collateral language, understand automatic debit authorization and save the final documents.

If the written terms are different from what you expected, stop and resolve the difference before accepting.

## FAQ

## What is the most important number to check before signing a personal loan?

There is no single number that tells the whole story. APR, finance charge, fees, payment amount, term and total of payments answer different parts of the cost question. Review them together.

## Is the monthly payment enough to compare two loan offers?

No. A lower payment can result from a longer term and may lead to a higher total repayment. Compare the full cost and term as well as the monthly obligation.

## Does CashPath write the loan agreement?

No. CashPath is not a lender. Any credit agreement comes from the provider offering the financial product.

## Should I keep a copy of my loan agreement?

Yes. Keep the final agreement, disclosures, payment schedule, provider contact information and any payment authorization for your records.

## Does every personal loan have the same Truth in Lending disclosures?

No. Regulation Z requirements depend on the type of credit and transaction, and disclosures are provided as applicable. Do not assume every product uses an identical form.
