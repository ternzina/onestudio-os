# Personal Loan Hardship or Payment Assistance: What to Ask Before You Miss a Payment

**Important:** CashPath is a personal-loan request and referral service, not a lender, loan servicer, debt-settlement company, credit counselor, or financial adviser. CashPath cannot pause, defer, extend, modify, forgive, refinance, or otherwise change a provider agreement. Hardship options are not guaranteed and vary by lender, servicer, contract, account status, state law, and individual circumstances. This page is general U.S. educational information, not legal, tax, or individualized financial advice. CashPath may receive compensation from advertising or referral relationships; see the [Advertiser Disclosure](/p/advertiser-disclosure).

## Short Answer

If you think you may not be able to make a scheduled personal-loan payment, the most useful first move is usually to contact the **actual lender or servicer as soon as possible** rather than waiting for the account to become more delinquent.

[The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-personal-installment-loan-en-2114/) says that when a borrower cannot make a personal-installment-loan payment, the borrower should contact the lender as soon as possible to understand available options. The CFPB notes that possible options **may** include deferment, forbearance, or a payment plan.

The word **may** matters.

A lender is not required by that statement to offer every option to every borrower. One company may offer an extension in a particular situation, another may offer a reduced-payment arrangement, another may offer no modification at all, and the terms can change according to the account and hardship.

Before agreeing to any change, ask what happens to the next due date, scheduled payment, interest, fees, loan term, past-due amount, autopay, credit reporting, and total amount you may ultimately repay.

Do not stop paying merely because you submitted a hardship request. Keep following the existing agreement unless the actual servicer tells you that the payment obligation has changed and you understand the new terms.

## Start With the Company That Actually Services the Loan

CashPath is not the company that services a personal loan after a consumer accepts an offer.

If you already have a loan, find the company responsible for the account by checking:

- the loan agreement;
- recent account statements;
- the provider's secure account portal;
- payment confirmations;
- official emails or letters received when the loan was opened; and
- verified contact information on the provider's official website.

Be careful with search ads, unsolicited calls, texts, or emails that claim to be the lender.

A person under payment stress can be an easy target for an impersonation scam. Do not give account credentials, Social Security information, bank login information, or payment details to a contact you have not independently verified.

If a loan has been transferred or a third-party servicer is handling payments, use the most recent verified servicing information rather than assuming the original company still controls the account.

## Contact the Servicer Before the Payment Problem Grows

A hardship conversation is generally more useful when it happens early.

[CFPB personal-installment-loan guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-personal-installment-loan-en-2114/) says borrowers who cannot make a payment should contact the lender as soon as possible. That does not guarantee a modification, but it gives the servicer a chance to explain what is actually available for the account.

When you contact the servicer, be ready to explain:

- why the normal scheduled payment is becoming difficult;
- whether the problem is temporary or likely to last;
- how much you can realistically pay now;
- when you expect your financial situation to change, if you know;
- whether you are already past due;
- whether another payment is scheduled through autopay; and
- what kind of temporary arrangement you are asking the servicer to consider.

The CFPB's current credit-card hardship guidance uses a similar communication framework: explain why the normal payment is not affordable, how much you can pay, when normal payments may resume, and what temporary amount or duration you are requesting.

Credit-card programs are not the same as personal-loan programs. The useful lesson is the communication structure, not the card-specific mechanics.

## Know What “Hardship Assistance” Can Mean

There is no single universal personal-loan hardship program.

Terms such as **extension**, **deferment**, **forbearance**, **payment plan**, **reduced payment**, and **due-date change** can describe different arrangements at different companies.

A [current KeyBank page](https://www.key.com/personal/services/borrowing/borrower-assistance/personal-loan-assistance.html), for example, describes personal-loan payment assistance that may include an extension or long-term hardship plan for some qualifying borrowers. Those are KeyBank-specific programs. Their eligibility, duration, payment treatment, and other terms should not be copied onto another lender or onto CashPath.

A safer approach is to treat the label as the beginning of a question.

Ask the servicer what the option means **for your exact loan**.

## Ask Whether the Payment Is Skipped, Moved, Reduced, or Still Due Later

A payment that disappears from this month's calendar has not necessarily disappeared from the debt.

Ask:

- Is the payment being skipped, postponed, split, or reduced?
- Does the postponed amount move to the end of the loan?
- Is it added to a future payment?
- Does the loan term get longer?
- Is a balloon or catch-up payment created?
- Does the principal balance change?
- Does interest continue to accrue during the modified period?
- Are any fees charged because of the arrangement?
- What exact date is the next payment due?

Do not rely on a phrase such as “you can skip this month” without understanding the mechanics.

A temporary payment pause can help cash flow while still increasing the amount of time or money needed to finish repayment.

## Ask What Happens to Interest

Interest treatment can be one of the most important details in a hardship arrangement.

Do not assume that a pause in payments means a pause in interest.

Ask the servicer:

- Does interest continue to accrue?
- Is interest calculated on the same balance during the assistance period?
- Is any interest capitalized or otherwise added to the amount owed?
- Is the interest rate changing?
- Is a lower rate temporary or permanent?
- Does the arrangement affect total repayment?

Only the actual agreement and hardship modification can answer those questions for a specific loan.

CashPath does not know or control a provider's interest treatment.

## Ask What Happens to Fees and Past-Due Amounts

If the account is already late, understand how the arrangement treats the existing delinquency.

Ask:

- Are any late fees already owed?
- Will new late fees continue during the arrangement?
- Does the hardship plan bring the account current?
- Is the past-due amount postponed, split, or still immediately due?
- Are returned-payment or other account fees involved?
- Will any waived fee return if the plan is not completed?

Do not assume a hardship plan erases an existing past-due balance.

Some arrangements may change payment timing without removing the underlying obligation.

## Ask How the Loan Term Changes

A lower temporary payment can be helpful while also extending repayment.

Before accepting, identify:

- the original scheduled payoff date;
- the new scheduled payoff date;
- the number of payments remaining;
- whether any additional payment is created; and
- whether the longer term changes total interest or total repayment.

A plan that reduces pressure this month may still cost more over time.

That does not automatically make the arrangement bad. It simply means the consumer should compare the immediate cash-flow benefit with the changed long-term obligation.

## Ask About Credit Reporting Before You Agree

Do not guess how a hardship arrangement will appear on a credit report.

Ask the servicer directly:

- Will the account be reported as current while the arrangement is followed?
- How will an existing delinquency be reported?
- Does entering the plan change the account status?
- What happens if a modified payment is made late?
- Will any notation or special status appear?
- When will ordinary reporting resume?

Get the answer from the company responsible for the account.

CashPath should not promise that a deferment, extension, or forbearance will have no effect on a credit report or score.

Credit reporting can depend on the account history, reporting practices, agreement, and whether the borrower follows the modified plan.

## Check Autopay Before the Due Date

Autopay can create an avoidable problem if a hardship request and an automatic withdrawal are happening at the same time.

If a payment is scheduled automatically, ask:

- Is the existing automatic payment still scheduled?
- Will the servicer cancel or change it?
- Do you need to change anything in the account portal?
- If the payment amount changes, when does the new amount take effect?
- What happens to any autopay-related rate discount or account benefit?

Do not cancel a legitimate scheduled payment on your own merely because a hardship request is pending unless you understand what the servicer has instructed and what the agreement requires.

You want the written modification and the payment system to match.

## Get the New Terms in Writing

A hardship arrangement is important enough to document.

Before relying on a changed payment schedule, ask for written confirmation that identifies:

- the effective date;
- the modified payment amount;
- the dates the modified payments are due;
- the duration of the arrangement;
- interest treatment;
- fee treatment;
- the new loan term or payoff schedule if changed;
- how past-due amounts are handled;
- any conditions for remaining in the plan; and
- what happens when the temporary period ends.

Save the confirmation with the original loan documents.

If the written terms are different from what you understood on the phone, resolve the difference before assuming the modification is active.

## Do Not Stop Paying While Waiting for an Answer Unless the Servicer Confirms a Change

Submitting a hardship request is not the same thing as having a hardship plan.

If the servicer says the request needs review, ask what you are expected to pay while the review is pending.

Do not assume that an application pauses the contract.

If the normal due date arrives before a decision, confirm whether the normal payment is still required.

This is particularly important when an account is close to becoming late or more seriously delinquent.

## If You Already Missed a Payment

If the payment is already late, contact the servicer anyway.

Ask for the exact current amount due and the account status.

Useful questions include:

- What amount is currently past due?
- Has a late fee been assessed?
- Has the account been reported as late?
- Is a cure or catch-up amount required?
- Is a hardship option still available?
- Would a payment plan change the delinquency status?
- Has the account been transferred to collections?
- Who is authorized to accept payment now?

Do not pay an unfamiliar collector until you verify who they are and what debt they are attempting to collect.

[CFPB debt-collection guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/) explains that consumers can request validation information and have rights against harassment, deception, and certain abusive practices.

## If the Problem Is Temporary, Map the Recovery Date

A temporary hardship plan is most useful when there is a plausible point at which normal cash flow resumes.

Examples can include:

- a delayed paycheck;
- a temporary reduction in work hours;
- a short medical leave;
- a one-time emergency expense;
- a gap between jobs;
- a temporary family-care obligation; or
- another time-limited disruption.

Write down the expected recovery date and compare it with the proposed hardship period.

If the plan ends before the income problem ends, the arrangement may simply move the missed-payment risk a few weeks forward.

If the plan lasts much longer than needed, ask whether a shorter option exists and what it would cost.

## If the Problem Is Long-Term, Look Beyond a One-Month Extension

A payment extension can bridge a date problem. It cannot fix a budget that remains negative every month.

If the household cannot afford the loan payment for the foreseeable future, use the hardship conversation to learn the available options but also review the broader debt picture.

A nonprofit credit counselor may be able to help a consumer build a budget and discuss debt-management options.

[The CFPB describes credit counseling](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-credit-counseling-en-1451/) organizations as commonly nonprofit organizations that can advise consumers on money and debts and may organize debt management plans.

That is different from debt settlement.

A debt management plan usually seeks a structured repayment arrangement. Debt settlement generally seeks less-than-full repayment and can involve different risks, including missed payments while a settlement is pursued.

Do not treat those strategies as interchangeable.

## Watch for “Hardship Help” Scams

A person who is worried about missing a loan payment may be contacted by companies promising to fix the problem.

Be cautious if someone:

- guarantees that a lender will reduce or erase the debt;
- claims to have a special government personal-loan hardship program without verifiable evidence;
- demands sensitive financial information before identifying the company and debt;
- tells you to send payments to a new account without verified servicing documentation;
- tells you to stop communicating with the actual lender;
- promises that credit reporting will be removed;
- asks for payment by gift card, cryptocurrency, wire transfer, or another hard-to-reverse method; or
- pressures you to act before you can verify the offer.

Contact the lender or servicer using contact information you independently verify.

Urgency is a reason to verify more carefully, not a reason to verify less.

## Practical Example: A Temporary Income Gap

Suppose a borrower normally makes a $325 monthly payment, but work hours were temporarily reduced for six weeks.

The borrower can afford $175 this month and expects normal income to resume before the following month's due date.

A useful hardship conversation would not begin with “Can I skip my payment?”

It would begin with:

- the current due date;
- the amount the borrower can realistically pay;
- the expected return to normal income;
- the servicer's available options;
- how each option treats interest and fees;
- whether the loan term changes;
- how autopay will work; and
- what will be reported while the plan is active.

The dollar amounts are hypothetical. They do not represent a typical personal-loan payment or provider program.

The goal is to match the arrangement to the actual length of the hardship.

## Practical Example: A Longer-Term Budget Problem

Suppose a borrower has lost a primary source of income and does not know when it will be replaced.

A one-payment extension may provide breathing room, but the same full payment could become unaffordable again as soon as the extension ends.

The borrower should still contact the servicer promptly.

At the same time, the borrower may need a broader review of:

- essential living expenses;
- all secured and unsecured debts;
- available hardship programs across creditors;
- nonprofit credit counseling;
- whether assets or income changes affect the plan; and
- when legal advice may be appropriate.

A short hardship arrangement and a long-term debt strategy are different decisions.

## Questions to Ask the Servicer

Before relying on any personal-loan hardship arrangement, try to answer:

- What exact option is being offered?
- Is it an extension, deferment, forbearance, payment plan, due-date change, reduced payment, or something else?
- What amount is due now?
- What amount will be due next?
- When is the next due date?
- Does interest continue to accrue?
- Are any fees charged or waived?
- What happens to any existing past-due amount?
- Does the loan term become longer?
- Does total repayment change?
- How will the account be reported to consumer reporting companies?
- What happens to autopay?
- What conditions must be met to remain in the plan?
- What happens when the plan ends?
- Can the complete arrangement be provided in writing?
- Who should you contact if the written terms do not match the account portal?

If the servicer cannot clearly explain the arrangement, do not assume the label tells you enough.

## FAQ

**Do personal loans have hardship programs?**
Some lenders or servicers may offer hardship or payment-assistance options, but there is no universal program that every personal-loan borrower can use. The CFPB says possible options when a borrower cannot make a payment may include deferment, forbearance, or a payment plan. The actual availability and terms depend on the lender, servicer, agreement, and circumstances.

**Can I pause a personal loan payment?**
Possibly, but only if the company servicing the loan offers and approves an arrangement that changes the payment schedule. Do not simply skip a payment. Confirm the new due date, interest treatment, fees, term, credit reporting, and written terms.

**Does personal-loan forbearance stop interest?**
Do not assume so. Interest treatment varies. Ask the actual servicer whether interest continues, whether the rate changes, and how the arrangement affects total repayment.

**Will a hardship plan hurt my credit?**
CashPath cannot predict that. Ask how the servicer will report the account during and after the arrangement, especially if a payment is already late. The reporting outcome can depend on account history and the terms of the plan.

**Should I cancel autopay after requesting hardship help?**
Do not make that assumption. Ask the servicer whether the scheduled withdrawal is still active and what, if anything, you need to change. A pending hardship request does not automatically change the existing payment instruction.

**Can CashPath change my payment or negotiate with my lender?**
No. CashPath is a request and referral service, not a lender or servicer. Questions about an accepted loan, payment, balance, due date, hardship plan, or account modification belong to the company servicing the loan.

## Bottom Line

A personal-loan hardship program is not one standard product.

If a payment is becoming unaffordable, contact the actual lender or servicer early and ask what options, if any, exist for the account.

Then look past the label.

Understand the new payment, next due date, interest, fees, past-due treatment, loan term, total repayment, credit reporting, and autopay. Get the arrangement in writing before assuming the contract has changed.

A temporary extension can solve a timing problem. A persistent monthly deficit needs a broader plan.

And until the servicer confirms a modification, continue to treat the existing agreement as the controlling payment obligation.
