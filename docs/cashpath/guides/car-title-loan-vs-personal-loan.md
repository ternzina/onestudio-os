# Car Title Loan vs. Personal Loan: Start With What You Could Lose

## Short Answer

The most important difference between a car title loan and an unsecured personal loan is not the advertising headline. It is the collateral.

A car title loan uses a vehicle as security for the debt. If the borrower cannot repay under the contract and applicable law, the vehicle can be at risk of repossession.

An unsecured personal loan does not use the vehicle as collateral, but it can still be expensive, unavailable, or difficult to repay.

So start with this question:

**If this debt goes badly, could I afford to lose the vehicle I use for work, medical care, school, or family transportation?**

Then compare the actual APR, fees, payoff schedule, payment amount, total repayment, and consequences of default.

## How a Car Title Loan Works

FTC consumer guidance describes car title loans as short-term loans secured by the borrower’s vehicle title.

The borrower provides the title as part of the transaction and does not regain clear title until the debt and applicable charges are repaid according to the agreement.

Title-loan structures vary, but a borrower should identify:

- the amount received;
- the finance charge;
- the APR;
- every other fee or add-on;
- the due date or installment schedule;
- whether rollovers or renewals are permitted;
- what happens after a missed or partial payment;
- when repossession can occur under the contract and state law; and
- what happens after a repossessed vehicle is sold.

Do not rely on the phrase “borrow against your car” as a complete explanation.

## Why the Vehicle-at-Risk Question Comes First

For many households, a vehicle is not a luxury.

It may be necessary to:

- get to work;
- take children to school or childcare;
- reach medical appointments;
- buy groceries;
- care for relatives; or
- live in an area without practical public transportation.

If losing the vehicle would also threaten income or essential transportation, the financial consequence of default can extend far beyond the loan balance.

That is why comparing only the monthly or daily cost can miss the biggest risk.

## FTC Guidance Warns About Repossession

The FTC says that if a borrower cannot repay a car title loan, the lender may repossess the vehicle.

The FTC also notes that title-loan contracts can involve added costs and, where allowed, rollovers that increase the amount paid while the original principal may remain outstanding.

Some lenders may also use GPS or starter-interrupt devices as part of repossession practices.

Those facts should lead to careful contract review, not panic or exaggerated claims.

Repossession is not something CashPath should describe as automatic after a single missed payment. Contract terms and state law matter.

## A Personal Loan Has a Different Risk Structure

A personal loan may be unsecured, depending on the provider and product.

If it is unsecured, the borrower is not pledging the vehicle as collateral for that loan.

But “unsecured” does not mean consequence-free.

A personal-loan agreement can still include:

- interest and APR;
- origination or other fees;
- scheduled payments;
- late or returned-payment fees;
- collection activity after default;
- credit reporting; and
- legal remedies available to the creditor under the agreement and law.

CashPath does not promise that a personal loan is safer, cheaper, or easier to obtain for every borrower.

## Compare the Payoff Timing

A title loan may have a short payoff window or another repayment structure depending on the product and state.

A personal loan may use scheduled installment payments over a longer term.

The key is not simply “short vs. long.”

Ask:

- When is the full balance due?
- Is the payment mostly fees, principal, or both?
- Does principal decline with each payment?
- Is there a balloon or large final payment?
- Can the loan be rolled over, renewed, or refinanced?
- What would that extension cost?
- How long would the debt remain outstanding if I follow the agreement exactly?

A small-looking short-term fee can become expensive if the debt repeatedly renews.

A longer installment loan can also cost more in total if repayment is stretched out.

## Use APR and Dollar Cost Together

FTC guidance currently gives an example of a title loan with a 25% monthly finance fee and explains that this can translate to an APR of about 300%.

That is a consumer-education example, not a universal title-loan rate.

Do not assume every title loan has that fee, and do not assume a personal loan will have a lower APR.

For the actual title-loan agreement, write down:

- principal or amount financed;
- cash actually received;
- finance charge;
- APR;
- added fees;
- add-on products;
- due date;
- total amount needed to satisfy the loan on time; and
- cost of any permitted renewal or rollover.

For the actual personal-loan offer, write down:

- amount offered;
- net proceeds;
- APR;
- fees;
- scheduled payment;
- term;
- total repayment; and
- late or missed-payment provisions.

Compare actual documents, not national averages. CashPath’s [Rates & Fees](/p/rates-fees) page explains the main cost fields, and the [loan-offer comparison guide](/p/how-to-compare-personal-loan-offers) provides a consistent checklist.

## Rollovers Can Make a Short Loan Stay Expensive

FTC guidance warns that when a title loan is rolled over, new fees can be added while the borrower may still owe the original amount borrowed.

If a rollover is offered, ask:

- What fee is due immediately?
- How much principal will remain?
- Does the new fee reduce principal at all?
- What will I owe after the rollover?
- How many times can the contract or state law allow renewal?
- Does a new agreement replace the old one?
- Does the vehicle remain subject to the lien the entire time?

Do not treat “I can renew if needed” as a repayment plan.

The better question is whether the original payoff amount fits the budget on the original due date.

## Look for Add-Ons and Extra Charges

FTC guidance notes that title-loan costs can include charges beyond the headline finance fee and can sometimes include add-on products.

Read for:

- processing charges;
- document fees;
- origination charges;
- roadside-service plans or other add-ons;
- lien or title-related charges;
- late fees;
- repossession-related charges; and
- any other amount listed in the agreement.

That does not mean every title lender charges every fee.

The point is to identify the total obligation in the actual contract.

## State Law Matters

Car title lending is regulated differently across states.

Rules can affect whether title lending is available, what terms are permitted, how repossession works, and what happens after a vehicle is sold.

Do not rely on a national blog post for a state-specific legal conclusion.

Before signing, verify:

- whether the lender is authorized to operate where you live;
- what state law applies to the contract;
- what disclosures are required;
- what repossession rights and notices apply; and
- whether there are complaint or regulator resources in your state.

If the contract is unclear, consider contacting the state attorney general or state financial regulator before proceeding.

## What Happens If the Vehicle Is Repossessed?

The answer depends on the contract and state law.

FTC guidance says a lender may sell a repossessed vehicle. It also notes that state rules can differ regarding sale proceeds.

Do not assume:

- the debt automatically disappears after repossession;
- the borrower always receives surplus sale proceeds;
- the lender always keeps a surplus;
- a deficiency is always collectible; or
- the same notice period applies everywhere.

Those are state- and contract-specific questions.

## Before a Title Loan, Check Whether the Bill Itself Can Move

Urgent borrowing is often driven by another bill.

Before pledging a vehicle, ask the company you owe whether it offers:

- a due-date change;
- a payment arrangement;
- a hardship plan;
- a partial-payment option;
- a short extension; or
- another documented accommodation.

The FTC encourages consumers who are struggling with bills to contact creditors and ask about payment arrangements.

An extension on the underlying bill may be less disruptive than creating a new vehicle-secured debt, but the creditor is not required to offer any particular accommodation.

## Other Alternatives Worth Checking

Depending on the situation, alternatives may include:

- a bank or credit union small-dollar product;
- an unsecured personal loan;
- help from family or friends;
- an employer or community assistance program;
- a payment plan with the original creditor;
- credit counseling for a broader debt problem; or
- another lawful option available in the borrower’s state.

None is automatically available or better.

Compare the real cost and risk.

## Example: The Cheapest-Looking Payment Is Not the Whole Decision

Suppose a borrower needs urgent cash and owns a vehicle that is essential for commuting to work.

A title-loan offer promises quick access to money but places the vehicle at risk under the loan agreement.

A separate personal-loan offer, if available, does not use the vehicle as collateral but has its own APR, fees, and repayment schedule.

The borrower should compare:

- the amount actually received;
- APR and all fees;
- the time allowed for repayment;
- total repayment;
- whether renewal is likely to be needed;
- missed-payment consequences; and
- the household impact if the car is lost.

The final factor can be more important than a small difference in payment size.

## Questions to Ask Before Signing a Car Title Loan

Ask the title lender:

- What exact amount will I receive?
- What is the APR?
- What is the finance charge in dollars?
- What other fees or add-ons apply?
- What is the exact payoff amount on the due date?
- Does each payment reduce principal?
- Can the loan roll over or renew?
- What would one renewal cost?
- When can repossession begin?
- What notices will I receive?
- Are GPS or starter-interrupt devices used?
- What happens after the vehicle is sold?
- Which state regulator licenses or supervises this lender?

Get the answers in the contract or required disclosures.

## Questions to Ask Before Taking a Personal Loan Instead

If comparing a personal loan, ask:

- Is the loan secured or unsecured?
- What are the APR and all fees?
- What are the net proceeds?
- What is the scheduled payment?
- What is the repayment term?
- What is total repayment?
- Does the provider permit my intended use?
- What happens after a late or missed payment?
- Is the payment realistic after essential monthly expenses?

Do not choose it solely because it avoids vehicle collateral.

## FAQ

**What is the biggest difference between a car title loan and an unsecured personal loan?**
A title loan uses the vehicle as collateral. An unsecured personal loan does not pledge the vehicle to secure that debt. Both can still carry substantial costs and consequences.

**Can a title lender take my car?**
FTC guidance says a title lender may repossess the vehicle if the borrower cannot repay. The timing, notices, and other rights depend on the contract and state law.

**Are all title loans 300% APR?**
No. The FTC gives examples showing how a 25% monthly finance fee can translate to about 300% APR, but that is not a universal rate for every title loan. Use the APR and fees disclosed for the actual contract.

**Is a personal loan always cheaper than a title loan?**
No. Compare the actual APR, fees, repayment term, total repayment, and collateral risk. CashPath does not promise a personal loan will be available or lower-cost.

## Bottom Line

A car title loan puts a specific asset at risk: the vehicle.

That can turn a borrowing problem into a transportation and income problem if repayment fails.

A personal loan may avoid that particular collateral risk when it is unsecured, but it still needs a full cost and budget review.

Compare the actual contract, payoff timing, APR, dollar fees, rollover mechanics, state-law consequences, and the real household value of keeping the vehicle.
