# What Happens If You Miss a Personal Loan Payment?

If you miss a personal loan payment, the consequences depend on how late the payment is, what your agreement says, what the lender or servicer does, and applicable law. Possible effects can include a late charge if permitted, negative information being furnished to credit reporting companies, delinquency or default under the agreement, collection activity, and additional risk to pledged collateral if the loan is secured.

CashPath is not your lender or loan servicer and cannot change a due date, waive a fee, change credit-reporting treatment, offer a hardship plan, or control collection activity. For account-specific information, use the verified contact details for the company that owns or services your loan.

## If you know before the due date that you cannot pay

Act before the missed payment if you can. Start by checking the current statement, account portal, and signed agreement rather than relying on memory.

Confirm:

- The due date.
- The scheduled payment amount.
- The payment method and whether autopay is active.
- Whether the agreement describes a grace period.
- The late-payment provisions.
- The company currently servicing the loan.

Then contact the lender or servicer and explain that you may have difficulty making the scheduled payment. Ask what options, if any, are available for your account. Depending on the provider and circumstances, an accommodation might involve a due-date change, temporary payment arrangement, deferment, forbearance, or another hardship option. Availability is not guaranteed.

## Ask how any hardship option changes the math

A skipped, delayed, or reduced payment is not automatically free. Before agreeing to an accommodation, ask how it changes the loan.

Questions to ask include:

- Will interest continue to accrue?
- Will the repayment term change?
- Will a payment be moved to a later date?
- Will a fee apply?
- How will the account be reported to credit reporting companies?
- Does the accommodation change the payoff amount?

If the lender or servicer offers an arrangement, ask for written confirmation or save the secure message or email that describes the new terms.

## What if the payment is already late?

Do not assume the situation is irreversible. Contact the lender or servicer and ask what amount is currently required to bring the account up to date if that is your goal.

Also ask whether:

- A late charge has been assessed.
- Another scheduled payment is approaching.
- Partial payment is accepted and how it would be applied.
- The account has been or may be reported to a credit reporting company.
- A hardship option is still available.
- Collection activity has begun or the account has been transferred.

A recently late payment and an account that has progressed into default or collections are not the same situation. Avoid relying on a generic internet timeline.

## Can a missed personal loan payment affect your credit?

Yes, it can. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's model notice about furnishing negative information states that late payments, missed payments, or other defaults may be reflected in a credit report.

That does not mean every provider reports at the same time or to every nationwide credit reporting company. A universal "30/60/90-day" schedule should not be treated as a rule for every personal loan.

If credit reporting matters to your situation, ask the lender or servicer what it reports and review your own credit reports for accuracy.

See the CFPB's [model notices of furnishing negative information](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1022/B/) for the underlying consumer-reporting language.

## Late, delinquent, default, and collections are not interchangeable

A late payment generally means a scheduled payment was not received as required by the agreement. A lender may charge a late fee if the agreement and applicable law allow it.

Delinquency generally describes an account that is past due, but providers can use different servicing stages and terminology.

Default is a more serious status triggered under the agreement after specified failures or events. The exact point at which a personal loan is considered in default is not universal.

If an unpaid account progresses far enough, collection activity can occur or the debt can be transferred or sold depending on the provider and circumstances. Keep records and verify anyone who contacts you about the debt before sending payment or sensitive information.

## Could you be charged a late fee?

Possibly. For covered closed-end consumer credit, Regulation Z includes disclosure requirements for certain charges imposed because an installment was not paid when due. The actual amount, timing, and availability of any late charge depend on the agreement and applicable law.

CashPath does not set provider late fees. Review the signed disclosures for whether a late fee exists, how it is calculated, when it may be charged, and whether a grace period applies.

The current Regulation Z rule is available at the CFPB's [§ 1026.18 Content of disclosures](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/18/).

## What if the loan is secured?

A secured loan is backed by collateral. If a borrower defaults, the lender may have rights against the pledged collateral under the agreement and applicable law.

That makes early communication especially important when the pledged asset is important to daily life. Do not assume the same repossession, cure, or notice rules apply to every type of collateral or every state.

## Seven practical actions after a missed payment

- Verify that the payment actually failed by checking both the lender portal and your bank account.
- Contact the lender or servicer through verified contact information.
- Ask for the exact amount currently due rather than relying on an older statement.
- Ask whether any hardship option is available for your account.
- Confirm how the account has been or may be reported to credit reporting companies.
- Keep statements, messages, confirmation numbers, and copies of any modified agreement.
- Rebuild the next month's budget so a catch-up payment does not immediately create another shortfall.

When a payment already strains the household budget, CashPath's [Responsible Lending](/p/responsible-lending) page offers general reminders about repayment ability and alternatives.

## What not to do after a missed payment

Do not ignore the lender or servicer. Silence does not pause contractual deadlines or servicing activity.

Do not assume another loan is automatically the solution. New borrowing can add another fee structure, another application, and another repayment obligation. Compare the complete cost and the reason for the shortfall before considering replacement debt.

Do not pay an unfamiliar company simply because it claims it can erase a late payment or guarantee a new loan. Verify the company and review current Federal Trade Commission guidance if someone demands money in exchange for promised credit.

Do not rely on one generic default timeline. Your agreement and verified lender or servicer are the first sources for your account.

## What if you cannot afford the payment long term?

If the problem is ongoing rather than temporary, a one-month accommodation may only move the pressure forward. Build a basic cash-flow snapshot using reliable take-home income, essential expenses, minimum debt obligations, and a realistic reserve for irregular expenses.

If the result is consistently negative, options to investigate can include contacting creditors about hardship arrangements, reviewing whether a refinance is actually available and mathematically useful, prioritizing essential expenses, speaking with a legitimate nonprofit credit counselor, or seeking qualified legal or financial help for a serious debt situation.

CashPath is not a credit-counseling, debt-settlement, or debt-relief service.

## What if refinancing comes up as an option?

Refinancing replaces an existing loan with a new loan. It is not an automatic hardship solution and approval or funding is not guaranteed.

If you compare a refinance, look at the current payoff amount, remaining term, current APR and costs, new APR, new fees, new repayment term, scheduled payment, and total remaining cost under both paths. A lower monthly payment can result from a longer term and can increase total cost.

If refinancing is one of the options you are considering, compare the full new obligation rather than using the lower monthly payment alone as the decision rule.

## Common questions after a missed payment

**Will one missed personal loan payment ruin my credit?**

A missed payment can affect your credit if negative information is furnished to credit reporting companies, but timing and score effects are not universal. Ask your provider about its reporting practices and review your credit reports for accuracy.

**How many days late can I be before a fee is charged?**

There is no universal answer for every personal loan. Check the late-payment section of your agreement and any applicable grace-period language.

**Is a personal loan automatically in default after 90 days?**

Do not assume that. Default timing is controlled by the agreement, provider practices, and applicable law.

**Can CashPath move my due date or waive a late fee?**

No. CashPath is not the lender or servicer and cannot change an existing account.

## The bottom line

A missed personal loan payment can lead to fees, negative credit reporting, delinquency, default, collection activity, or collateral risk, but the exact path depends on the provider, agreement, and applicable law.

The strongest first response is practical: read the agreement, verify what is due, contact the real lender or servicer promptly, ask about available hardship options, document any arrangement, and rebuild the budget for the next payment.
