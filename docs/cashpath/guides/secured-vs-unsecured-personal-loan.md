## Short Answer

The most important difference between a **secured** and **unsecured** personal loan is not the word printed in the advertisement. It is whether specific property is legally tied to repayment.

A secured loan gives the creditor a security interest in identified collateral under the agreement and applicable law. If the borrower defaults, the creditor may have rights against that collateral in addition to other remedies allowed by law.

An unsecured loan does not give the creditor a specific asset as collateral for that obligation. That does **not** mean the debt becomes optional or that nothing can happen after default. Missed payments can still lead to collection activity, credit reporting, a lawsuit, and, after the required legal process, judgment remedies.

The [FDIC's consumer-loan guidance](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/loans) explains the basic distinction: a secured loan involves pledged collateral, while installment and revolving credit can be either secured or unsecured. It also warns that when collateral is sold after default, a borrower may still be responsible for a remaining balance if the proceeds do not fully satisfy the debt.

For a consumer comparing offers, the first question should be:

**What asset, if any, am I putting at risk, and where is that stated in the agreement?**

Only after answering that question should you compare APR, fees, payment schedule, total repayment, term, and other features.

## What “Secured” Means in a Personal-Loan Agreement

A loan is secured when the creditor has a legally recognized interest in property that secures the obligation.

Regulation Z defines a **security interest** as an interest in property that secures performance of a consumer-credit obligation and is recognized by state or federal law. The exact legal rights depend on the agreement, the type of collateral, and applicable law.

For covered closed-end consumer credit, [Regulation Z § 1026.18](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/18/) requires disclosure of the fact that the creditor has or will acquire a security interest in property purchased as part of the transaction or in other property identified by item or type, when that requirement applies.

That means a consumer should not have to guess whether a covered transaction is secured.

Look for words such as:

- security interest;
- collateral;
- pledge;
- lien;
- secured by;
- property subject to the creditor's interest; or
- other contract language identifying an asset tied to repayment.

The specific wording can vary.

Do not rely only on a salesperson, comparison page, or search snippet saying a loan is “secured.” Read the provider's actual disclosure and agreement.

## What “Unsecured” Means

An unsecured personal loan does not give the creditor a security interest in a specific asset as collateral for that obligation.

That distinction matters because the creditor does not begin with a contractual right to take an identified pledged asset simply because a payment is missed.

But unsecured does **not** mean consequence-free.

Depending on the agreement and applicable law, nonpayment can still result in:

- late or other charges permitted by the agreement and law;
- delinquency or default;
- credit reporting;
- collection activity;
- charge-off;
- assignment or sale of the debt;
- a lawsuit;
- a judgment; and
- post-judgment remedies permitted by law.

For later collection stages, see [Personal Loan Charge-Off vs. Collections](/p/personal-loan-charge-off-vs-collections) and [Can a Personal Loan Creditor Garnish Your Wages, Bank Account, or Benefits?](/p/personal-loan-wage-garnishment-bank-account-benefits).

The important distinction here is simpler:

**Unsecured means no specific collateral secures the obligation. It does not mean the creditor has no legal remedies.**

## Collateral Changes the Risk, Not Just the Application

Consumers often focus on whether collateral might help them qualify or change a price.

That can miss the larger decision.

Collateral is an asset you could lose or have tied up if the obligation goes badly.

Depending on the product, collateral could include property such as:

- a vehicle;
- money in a savings account;
- a certificate of deposit;
- securities or other eligible financial assets; or
- another type of property specifically accepted by the creditor and permitted by law.

Not every creditor accepts every asset.

Do not assume that because an asset has value, a particular provider will accept it.

Do not assume that CashPath can determine whether an asset qualifies.

And do not confuse a general secured personal loan with specialized products that have their own structures and risks, such as:

- a mortgage;
- a home-equity loan;
- a home equity line of credit;
- a vehicle-purchase loan;
- a vehicle-title loan; or
- a pawn transaction.

Those products can be secured too, but their legal and practical mechanics can be very different.

## Ask Exactly What Property Secures the Loan

If an offer is described as secured, identify the collateral before comparing anything else.

Useful questions include:

- What property is listed as collateral?
- Is the collateral mine, jointly owned, or owned by someone else?
- Does the agreement identify the collateral by item or type?
- Does any property from an older loan also secure this obligation?
- Is a deposit account being pledged?
- Is there a lien or filing associated with the collateral?
- Does the agreement restrict sale or transfer of the asset?
- What events count as default?
- What rights does the creditor claim after default?
- What costs can be added if collateral must be recovered or sold?
- Could I still owe money after the collateral is sold?

Regulation Z's official commentary recognizes that security interests can be described with terms such as “pledge,” “lien,” or “mortgage,” and it discusses situations in which collateral securing an earlier obligation may also secure a new one.

If the agreement contains language you do not understand, get clarification before accepting.

## A Security Interest Is More Important Than a Marketing Label

A provider can market a product with simple language.

The contract is where the actual financial obligation lives.

A page might say:

- “secured option”;
- “savings-secured”;
- “share-secured”;
- “vehicle-secured”; or
- “collateral loan.”

Those labels can help you orient yourself, but they are not enough to understand the deal.

The agreement should answer what property is involved and what rights are created.

For covered closed-end transactions, Regulation Z § 1026.18(m) requires an applicable security-interest disclosure. The official commentary says nonpurchase-money collateral generally must be identified by item or type.

The practical consumer rule is:

**Find the property, not just the adjective.**

## Does a Secured Personal Loan Always Have a Lower APR?

There is no universal rule that a secured personal loan will always be cheaper for every borrower.

Collateral can reduce a creditor's exposure to loss, so a provider may price or underwrite secured credit differently from unsecured credit.

But the actual result depends on the specific offer.

APR and cost can be affected by provider pricing, the loan amount, repayment term, fees, borrower information, the collateral structure, state law, and other factors.

There is no universal secured or unsecured APR range that can reliably describe every provider and borrower.

Instead, compare the actual disclosures.

For any offer, review:

- APR;
- interest rate;
- finance charge;
- amount financed;
- fees;
- payment schedule;
- repayment term;
- total of payments or other applicable total-cost disclosure;
- security-interest language;
- late-payment provisions;
- default provisions; and
- prepayment provisions where applicable.

CashPath's [Rates & Fees page](/p/rates-fees) explains why APR, fees, scheduled payment, term, and total repayment should be reviewed together.

## Do Not Assume Secured Means Easier Approval

Collateral does not create guaranteed approval.

A provider applies its own eligibility, verification, underwriting, documentation, and collateral rules to the specific product and application. Those rules can differ by provider and transaction.

CashPath does not set those rules and does not estimate approval odds. The [About CashPath page](/p/about) explains that participating providers, not CashPath, determine eligibility, approval, offered amount, APR, fees, and other terms.

## Do Not Assume Unsecured Means “Nothing to Lose”

With an unsecured loan, no identified asset secures the obligation.

That is an important protection against direct collateral risk.

But the debt can still matter to your finances.

If payments stop, an unsecured creditor may use lawful collection methods.

A common mistake is to hear “no collateral” and translate it into:

A common mistake is to assume that **the lender cannot do anything if you stop paying**.

That is false.

For an ordinary private unsecured debt, a creditor generally needs to follow the required legal process before using judgment remedies such as ordinary wage garnishment.

That process is different from enforcing a security interest in pledged collateral.

The distinction matters:

- **secured-default risk** can involve the collateral identified in the agreement; while
- **unsecured-default risk** can involve collections, litigation, and judgment enforcement rather than a pre-existing claim to a specific pledged asset.

Neither should be treated casually.

## What Can Happen to Collateral After Default?

The exact process depends on the collateral, agreement, state law, and other facts.

Do not assume that one missed payment means the asset is immediately seized.

A contract should define default and the creditor's rights.

Applicable law may impose notice, timing, repossession, sale, accounting, or other requirements.

The FDIC's consumer guidance gives the broad principle: when a borrower cannot repay a secured loan as agreed, the lender may take collateral and use it to recover some or all of the money owed.

That does not answer every case.

A consumer should review:

- the event that triggers default;
- any right to cure;
- required notices;
- repossession or control rights;
- sale procedures;
- costs that may be charged;
- how sale proceeds are applied; and
- whether a deficiency balance can remain.

A general educational page cannot determine whether a specific creditor has complied with the law.

If a high-value asset is at risk and default has occurred, legal advice may be appropriate.

## Can You Still Owe Money After Collateral Is Sold?

Potentially, yes.

The FDIC warns that a borrower may still be responsible for a remaining balance if collateral does not sell for enough to repay the debt.

Whether a deficiency is allowed, how it is calculated, and what procedures apply can depend on the type of transaction and state law.

Do not make either of these assumptions:

- “They took the collateral, so the debt must be finished.”
- “If the asset is worth more than the balance, I will definitely receive a particular amount back.”

The provider's accounting, the sale process, permitted costs, applicable law, and the actual proceeds matter.

If collateral has been taken or sold, obtain the written account history and sale information available to you.

## A Savings-Secured Loan Can Still Put Real Money at Risk

Collateral does not have to be a car or other physical property.

Some financial institutions offer credit secured by funds held in a savings account, share account, or certificate.

If your own deposit is pledged, the risk may feel less dramatic because nobody is towing a vehicle.

But the money can still be restricted or exposed under the agreement.

Before accepting a deposit-secured obligation, confirm:

- which account or certificate is pledged;
- how much of it is restricted;
- whether you can withdraw the funds;
- whether the pledged amount changes as the balance is repaid;
- whether the creditor has a contractual right to apply the deposit after default;
- what happens to interest or dividends on the deposit; and
- when the restriction is released.

Do not assume all savings-secured products work the same way.

## Vehicle Collateral Can Create a Transportation Risk

If a vehicle secures a personal loan, the decision is not only financial.

Ask what would happen to your daily life if that vehicle became unavailable.

Consider:

- getting to work;
- transporting children;
- medical appointments;
- school;
- caregiving;
- access to groceries;
- alternative transportation cost; and
- whether another household vehicle exists.

A lower quoted payment or APR can be less attractive if the collateral is essential to earning income or maintaining basic mobility.

This does not mean vehicle-secured credit is always a bad decision.

It means the asset risk belongs in the comparison.

## Compare the Consequences of Default Before Comparing the Benefits

A loan comparison often starts with the best-case scenario:

A useful best-case question is: **Which offer costs less if everything goes according to plan?**

Also ask:

Also ask: **What happens if my income drops, I miss payments, or I need to exit the obligation early?**

For a secured offer, review:

- collateral enforcement rights;
- default triggers;
- cure rights if any;
- repossession or liquidation procedures;
- possible deficiency;
- restrictions on selling the collateral; and
- insurance or maintenance obligations tied to the asset.

For an unsecured offer, review:

- late-payment provisions;
- collection rights;
- acceleration language;
- credit-reporting consequences;
- lawsuit risk; and
- post-judgment remedies under applicable law.

Default is not the expected outcome.

But understanding it before signing is part of understanding the product.

## How to Compare a Secured and Unsecured Offer Without Guessing

Do not compare “secured” and “unsecured” in the abstract when you have actual offers.

Compare the documents side by side.

Start with the amount.

Are both offers actually providing the same amount of usable funds?

Then compare the cost.

Review:

- APR;
- finance charge;
- fees;
- payment amount;
- number and timing of payments;
- repayment term;
- total repayment or total of payments where disclosed.

Then compare the risk.

For the secured offer:

- identify the collateral;
- estimate how essential that asset is to your household;
- read the creditor's security-interest and default rights;
- determine whether another lien already exists.

For the unsecured offer:

- confirm that no specific collateral secures the obligation;
- read default and collection provisions;
- understand the consequences of missed payments.

Finally, compare flexibility.

Look for:

- prepayment terms;
- due-date rules;
- hardship or modification policies if disclosed;
- automatic-payment requirements if any;
- account restrictions; and
- whether fees change the economics of the offer.

## Example: The “Cheaper” Offer Puts an Essential Asset at Risk

Suppose a consumer receives two hypothetical offers for the same requested amount.

One is unsecured.

The other is secured by the consumer's only working vehicle.

The secured offer has a lower quoted borrowing cost.

It would be easy to stop there and call it “better.”

But the decision should also account for:

- what the vehicle is worth to the household;
- whether losing it would threaten employment;
- whether transportation alternatives exist;
- the exact default and repossession language;
- any collateral-related fees;
- and whether the payment fits the budget with a margin for emergencies.

The better question is not:

The decision is not only: **Which APR is lower?**

It is:

A stronger question is: **Does the cost difference justify putting this specific asset at risk?**

The answer can be different for different households.

## Example: Collateral That Is Not Essential May Still Have Opportunity Cost

Suppose a consumer considers a loan secured by money in a certificate of deposit.

There may be no transportation risk.

But there can still be a tradeoff.

If the deposit is restricted while the loan is outstanding, the consumer may lose access to emergency savings or other financial flexibility.

Compare:

- the borrowing cost;
- the return earned on the deposit;
- access restrictions;
- the amount pledged;
- the release schedule;
- and the consequence of default.

Do not treat “cash-secured” as “risk-free.”

The asset is still part of the bargain.

## Example: An Unsecured Offer With a Lower Payment Can Still Cost More

A lower scheduled payment can come from a longer repayment term.

That is true whether the loan is secured or unsecured.

Imagine two offers where the unsecured loan has a smaller monthly payment because repayment extends over a longer period.

A consumer who looks only at the payment may miss a higher total repayment.

Compare the entire disclosure.

CashPath's [Rates & Fees page](/p/rates-fees) explains why a smaller payment is not automatically a cheaper obligation.

## Check Whether the Asset Already Has a Lien

An asset can already be subject to another creditor's interest.

That can affect whether it is eligible collateral and what rights different creditors may have.

Examples include:

- a vehicle that still has an auto-loan lien;
- a deposit already pledged for another obligation; or
- property involved in a prior secured transaction.

Do not tell a provider you own an asset “free and clear” if you are unsure.

Review ownership records and existing agreements.

If a provider proposes cross-collateralization or says collateral from another account also secures the new obligation, read that language carefully.

Regulation Z's official commentary specifically discusses “spreader,” “dragnet,” and cross-collateralization clauses in applicable disclosure contexts.

## Third-Party Collateral Requires Extra Care

Sometimes collateral can belong to someone other than the borrower.

Regulation Z's commentary recognizes that a credit obligation can be secured by property belonging to a third party.

That can create consequences for someone who is not receiving the loan proceeds.

Before another person pledges property:

- identify exactly what they are signing;
- confirm which debt the property secures;
- understand when the creditor can act against the collateral;
- determine whether the pledge continues for future obligations;
- and consider independent legal advice for significant property.

Do not treat pledging an asset as a casual favor.

## Secured vs. Unsecured Is Not the Same as Fixed vs. Variable Rate

These are different dimensions.

A loan can be:

- secured and fixed-rate;
- secured and variable-rate;
- unsecured and fixed-rate; or
- unsecured and variable-rate,

depending on the actual product and provider.

Do not assume security automatically determines rate structure.

For rate-change mechanics, see [Fixed vs. Variable Rate Personal Loan](/p/fixed-vs-variable-rate-personal-loan). This page focuses on collateral risk.

## Secured vs. Unsecured Is Not the Same as Installment vs. Revolving

These are also different dimensions.

The FDIC notes that installment and revolving loans can be either secured or unsecured.

A personal installment loan generally gives you a defined obligation to repay over time.

A line of credit is revolving/open-end access under its agreement.

For the lump-sum-versus-reusable-credit decision, see [Personal Loan vs. Personal Line of Credit](/p/personal-loan-vs-personal-line-of-credit).

Do not collapse these categories into one chart that makes every secured product look installment and every unsecured product look revolving.

Before accepting a secured obligation, locate each of these items in the provider's documents:

- creditor identity;
- amount financed;
- APR;
- finance charge;
- payment schedule;
- total of payments or applicable cost disclosure;
- collateral or security-interest description;
- late-payment provisions;
- default definition;
- acceleration rights;
- repossession or collateral-enforcement language;
- insurance requirements if any;
- filing or security-interest fees if any;
- prepayment terms; and
- contract reference for nonpayment and default.

Regulation Z § 1026.18 is the federal baseline for many closed-end consumer-credit disclosures, though the exact requirements depend on the transaction.

The provider documents, not a general CashPath article, control your actual obligation.

## What to Read Before Signing an Unsecured Personal Loan

The absence of collateral does not reduce the importance of the disclosures.

Review:

- creditor identity;
- amount financed;
- APR;
- finance charge;
- fees;
- payment schedule;
- repayment term;
- total repayment;
- late-payment provisions;
- default provisions;
- acceleration rights;
- collection costs if applicable; and
- prepayment terms.

Also confirm whether the agreement truly is unsecured.

Do not assume based only on the word “personal loan.”

## Questions to Ask Before Pledging Collateral

If a provider offers a secured option, pause long enough to answer these questions:

- Do I understand exactly what asset is being pledged?
- Do I need this asset for work, housing, transportation, health, or emergency savings?
- Is the asset jointly owned?
- Does someone else have rights in it?
- Is there an existing lien?
- Can I sell or transfer it while the loan is outstanding?
- What happens after a missed payment?
- Is there a cure period?
- How would repossession or liquidation work?
- Could I owe a deficiency afterward?
- What fees can be added?
- When is the lien or pledge released?
- Is the secured offer actually less expensive after all fees?
- Would an unsecured offer fit the budget without putting the asset at risk?
- Are lower-cost alternatives available?

If you cannot answer the collateral questions from the documents, do not treat the offer as fully understood.

## Alternatives to Pledging an Important Asset

Depending on the reason you need money, alternatives may include:

- asking the provider of the underlying expense about a payment plan;
- seeking a hardship arrangement on an existing bill;
- using savings without creating a new debt when appropriate;
- checking a local credit union for different products;
- delaying a nonessential expense;
- reducing the amount borrowed;
- selling an unneeded asset instead of pledging an essential one; or
- exploring community or assistance resources where relevant.

These alternatives are not automatically cheaper or available to everyone.

Compare the real terms.

## When a Secured Personal Loan May Deserve Extra Caution

Extra caution is reasonable when:

- the collateral is essential for employment;
- the collateral is your main emergency fund;
- the asset is jointly owned;
- another person is pledging the asset;
- the agreement is unclear about the property covered;
- the creditor claims a security interest in more property than expected;
- the loan refinances another secured obligation;
- the asset is already subject to a lien;
- default would create a major housing or transportation problem; or
- you are using new debt to solve an ongoing monthly budget deficit.

Caution does not mean automatic rejection.

It means the downside deserves to be understood before accepting the upside.

## When an Unsecured Personal Loan May Be Easier to Evaluate

An unsecured obligation can be simpler in one important way: there is no pledged asset to track.

That can remove questions about repossession, collateral valuation, lien release, and sale restrictions.

But the consumer still needs to understand:

- whether the payment is affordable;
- total repayment;
- fees;
- rate structure;
- credit consequences;
- default provisions; and
- collection remedies.

No collateral does not replace normal loan comparison.

## FAQ

## Is a personal loan always unsecured?

No.

Many personal loans are unsecured, but secured personal-loan products also exist.

Read the actual agreement and security-interest disclosure rather than assuming the label “personal loan” answers the question.

## Can a lender take my car if my personal loan is unsecured?

An unsecured personal loan does not give the creditor a security interest in a specific car under that loan agreement.

However, unpaid unsecured debt can still lead to a lawsuit and judgment, and judgment remedies vary by law.

That is different from a creditor enforcing a pre-existing security interest in a vehicle pledged as collateral.

## Is a secured personal loan always cheaper?

No.

A secured structure may be priced differently because collateral changes the creditor's risk, but there is no universal rate advantage that applies to every borrower or provider.

Compare the actual APR, fees, term, payment, total repayment, and collateral risk.

## Does collateral guarantee approval?

No.

A provider can still apply its own eligibility, verification, underwriting, documentation, and collateral criteria.

CashPath does not determine or predict approval.

## Can a lender take collateral immediately after one missed payment?

Do not assume that.

Default definitions and enforcement procedures depend on the agreement, the collateral, applicable law, and the facts.

Review the contract and required notices.

## If the lender repossesses collateral, is the loan automatically paid off?

Not necessarily.

The FDIC warns that a borrower may still owe a remaining balance if collateral does not sell for enough to repay the debt.

Deficiency rules and procedures vary.

## Can savings or a CD be collateral?

Some institutions offer deposit-secured credit, but product availability and terms vary.

If a deposit is pledged, confirm which funds are restricted, when they are released, and what the creditor can do after default.

## Is a secured personal loan the same as a title loan?

No.

A vehicle-title loan is a specialized secured product with its own legal and cost issues.

This guide covers the general secured-versus-unsecured personal-loan decision and should not substitute for title-loan-specific guidance.
